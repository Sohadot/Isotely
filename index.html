Equal Treatment.
Without Citizenship.

An ancient distinction. A global inquiry.

Isotely begins with a rare word rooted in the treatment of non-citizens. We use that historical distinction to investigate a precise modern question: when does the law afford equivalent treatment without conferring the same legal status?

Explore the meaningOur method

The defining distinction

Equal treatment is not the same as equal status.

Every comparison needs a named right or obligation, a legal-status comparator, a jurisdiction, conditions, and evidence.

01 / Meaning

The word came first.

Isotely draws on Greek isoteleia: historically bounded equal treatment of certain non-citizens, particularly in fiscal obligations or privileges, without granting citizenship as a whole.

A bounded equality — not an undifferentiated claim to all civic rights.

History is the foundation. Not the product claim.

The ancient institution and the modern method are distinct. We do not claim that isoteleia supplied modern immigration law, full political rights, or a ready-made system of worldwide legal comparison.

Our contribution is a proposed evidence discipline for comparing specific legal treatment across stated statuses. It must earn acceptance on its merits.

Historical interpretation requires scholarly source verification before formal publication. This page presents a working description, not a final historical authority.
02 / Method

Precisely where treatment meets status.

We avoid general country rankings. An Isotely reference record answers a limited, reproducible question and states what it cannot prove.

01 / IDENTIFY

Who is compared?

A defined non-citizen status; an explicit citizen or other stated comparator; a jurisdiction; eligibility conditions.

02 / ISOLATE

What treatment?

One specific right, obligation or legal effect — not an unsupported judgment about equality in general.

03 / PROVE

On what basis?

Primary legal provisions, exceptions, time scope, verification status and a candid account of uncertainty.

Possible findings: equal on the defined rule · conditionally equal · similar effect with different legal basis · unequal · unresolved. Legal interpretation and practical outcomes remain separate.

03 / Evidence

Three records. No inflated claims.

Our initial Canada pilot tests three different relationship types between permanent residents and citizens. It is a constrained research demonstration, not a globally validated dataset or professional legal opinion.

ISO-LP-CA-001 · Federal / CanadaPursuing a livelihood across provinces
Primary-text reading · Not legally reviewed

Canadian Charter §6(2)(b) expressly addresses both citizens and permanent residents, subject to §6(3) and §6(4). This is not blanket equality across occupations or public benefits.

Primary source: Canadian Charter, §6.

ISO-LP-CA-002 · Federal / CanadaEntry and continued residence
Different legal bases · Not legally reviewed

Charter §6(1) protects citizens' entry and residence. Permanent residents have statutory rights under IRPA §27 subject to other legal provisions including residence obligations. Different source of protection does not mean no right.

Primary sources: Charter §6; IRPA §27; IRPA §28.

ISO-LP-CA-003 · Federal / CanadaVoting in federal elections
Distinct treatment · Not legally reviewed

Canadian citizenship is required for federal voting; permanent residence alone does not provide this entitlement. This finding is only about federal elections, not every local election rule.

Official reference: Elections Canada — eligibility to vote.

Pilot prepared 24 October 2026. No qualified legal review, comprehensive current-law validation, jurisdictional expansion, or verified before/after statutory changes. Expand records only after review. Individual circumstances may change legal outcomes.
