## Asset Snapshot

### Short Strategic Summary

Isotely.com is a seven-letter `.com` built around the rare historical term connected to Greek *isoteleia* (ἰσοτέλεια). The historical concept concerned a bounded form of equal treatment for non-citizens rather than transformation into full citizens. The modern asset translates that structural distinction into a technical question for heterogeneous identity systems: when may a non-native identity receive functionally equivalent operational treatment for a declared purpose without inheriting the unrestricted privileges of the native identity?

The asset is being developed as a conceptual and reference-grade category artifact, not as a domain-for-sale surface and not as a generic IAM product. Its immediate objective is to establish a defensible definition, strict category boundary, controlled lexicon, one auditable reference case, and a machine-readable representation only after terminology has been tested against external technical vocabulary.

### Canonical Definition

> **Isotely is purpose-bounded operational parity across unlike identity classes.**

### Extended Definition

Isotely describes the condition in which a non-native identity can receive functionally equivalent operational treatment relative to a native identity for a defined purpose, without inheriting that identity's unrestricted privileges.

The core distinction is that **operational parity does not require permission equality**. Two unlike identities may hold different entitlements while remaining equivalent with respect to the effects required by a declared purpose, provided prohibited effects remain outside the parity boundary.

### Current Thesis

Modern identity systems increasingly need to govern unlike actors — humans, applications, service identities, and autonomous agents — inside environments whose permissions were not designed around identical identity classes. Authentication answers who an actor is. Authorization answers what an actor may do. Delegation answers whose authority an actor may exercise. Isotely addresses a different question: **whether an unlike identity can be treated as operationally equivalent for a specific purpose without inheriting the native identity's unrestricted privilege set.**

## Domain Strategic Assessment

### Strategic Verdict

**Strategically Strong**

### Native Strength

`Isotely` is short, memorable, pronounceable, and unusually rare while retaining a documented historical root rather than behaving like an arbitrary startup coinage. Its strongest strategic property is not rarity alone: the historical structure of bounded equal treatment provides a credible conceptual bridge into a modern technical problem. That relationship must be presented as a deliberate modern application of the historical structure, not as a claim that the ancient term already meant AI identity parity.

### Category and Sector Fit

The strongest fit is not generic IAM, authentication, authorization, or agent security. Those fields are already mature and crowded. The asset should occupy a narrower conceptual layer: purpose-bounded operational parity across heterogeneous identity classes. This allows Isotely to complement existing authorization and delegation systems rather than compete with them as another identity platform.

### Geographic Logic

Geography is not central to the modern asset. The Greek provenance supplies conceptual lineage, not geographic market positioning.

### Buildability

The asset can support a real digital property composed of a canonical doctrine, category boundary, lexicon, reference cases, machine-readable parity profiles, research notes, and eventually a reference implementation. The first build should remain static-first and evidence-first. No enterprise connector, identity provider, authorization engine, or certification surface is justified at this stage.

### Authority Potential

Authority can be earned if Isotely becomes precise enough that a practitioner can use the concept to distinguish operational effect from permission symmetry, and if every reference case can be independently audited from public technical documentation. The authority mechanism is therefore definition + boundary + evidence + versioned representation, not marketing volume.

### Strategic Risks

1. **Terminology collision:** established IAM vocabulary may already describe part or all of the proposed distinction under different terms.
2. **Standing ambiguity:** the word `standing` has established security meanings such as standing privilege and Zero Standing Privilege; it should not be used as the canonical category label without strong reason.
3. **Overreach:** calling Isotely a new standard before external vocabulary testing would weaken credibility.
4. **Historical overstatement:** ancient *isoteleia* must not be simplified into full civic equality.
5. **Category collapse:** if the concept cannot remain distinct from authorization, delegation, effective permissions, or least privilege, the category claim must be revised rather than defended rhetorically.
6. **Premature commercialization:** buyer-facing or acquisition language would lower the asset from reference artifact to marketed inventory.

### Assessment Conclusion

Isotely warrants a structured reference build. The concept is strong enough to formalize, but not yet mature enough to freeze a standard or claim independent category adoption. External terminology must feed the lexicon and draft representation before any `1.0` designation.

## Category Boundary

### Isotely concerns

The determination and representation of **purpose-bounded operational parity between actors belonging to unlike identity classes**.

### Isotely does not mean

- authentication;
- authorization protocol;
- identity provider;
- delegation protocol;
- Zero Standing Privilege;
- entitlement inventory;
- privilege-drift monitoring;
- agent discovery;
- agent capability scoring;
- generic least-privilege enforcement;
- compliance certification;
- legal standing;
- human/citizen status for machines.

### Boundary map

```text
WHO IS THE ACTOR?
Identity

CAN THE ACTOR PROVE THAT IDENTITY?
Authentication

WHAT ACTIONS ARE PERMITTED?
Authorization

ON WHOSE BEHALF OR MANDATE?
Delegation / mandate

---------------------------------------------
ISOTELY

Can an unlike identity receive functionally
 equivalent operational treatment for this
 declared purpose, inside this boundary,
 without inheriting unrestricted privilege?
---------------------------------------------

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS?
Execution / operational effect
```

### Boundary rule

If a proposed Isotely feature can be completely described as identity discovery, authentication, authorization, delegation, entitlement inventory, or drift detection without reference to cross-class purpose-bounded parity, it does not belong to Isotely's conceptual core.

## Foundation Principles

### P1 — Identity classes may differ

Parity does not require the native and non-native actors to belong to the same identity class.

### P2 — Purpose is mandatory

There is no valid claim of Isotely without a declared purpose. `Equivalent` in the abstract is too broad to be meaningful.

### P3 — Operational effect outranks permission symmetry

Different permission structures may produce the same allowed operational effect. The evaluation target is the declared effect set, not syntactic equality between entitlement lists.

### P4 — Privilege inheritance is not parity

Copying a human or native actor's full privilege set into an agent or non-native identity is not proof of Isotely. It may instead prove over-privilege.

### P5 — Prohibited effects are first-class

A parity claim is incomplete if it lists only what the non-native identity must be able to do. It must also specify effects that must remain impossible.

### P6 — Boundaries must be explicit

Time, repository, environment, resource, action class, review requirement, and other constraints may define the parity envelope.

### P7 — Evidence is required

A parity claim without evidence is an assertion, not a reference-grade result.

### P8 — Vocabulary remains revisable before 1.0

The canonical definition is fixed for the current foundation phase. Field names, mappings, and adjacent terminology remain provisional and may change in response to authoritative external vocabulary.

## Reference Case 001 — Human Developer → GitHub Coding Agent

### Status

**Foundation case — auditable from public GitHub documentation.**

This case is selected because GitHub exposes documented distinctions between OAuth Apps, GitHub Apps, and Agent Apps. GitHub currently describes Agent Apps as GitHub Apps that expose agents directly in GitHub workflows. GitHub also recommends GitHub Apps over OAuth Apps for many integrations because GitHub Apps use fine-grained permissions, repository selection, and short-lived tokens.

### Declared purpose

> A coding agent may inspect one designated repository, modify source code on a working branch, commit those changes, and open or update a pull request for human review.

This purpose deliberately excludes repository administration, unrestricted organization access, secret management, and bypass of human review.

### Native identity class

**Human developer** with ordinary developer access appropriate to the repository.

A human developer may possess a broad combination of permissions because the same identity performs many unrelated development and administrative tasks.

### Non-native identity class

**GitHub App / Agent App-backed coding agent.**

The candidate identity should receive only the permissions and repository reach necessary to produce the declared operational effects.

### Required operational effects

The exact endpoint-to-permission mapping must be verified during implementation, but the intended effect set is:

1. read repository contents needed for the task;
2. create or update a task-specific branch where permitted;
3. write task-scoped source changes;
4. create commits for those changes;
5. read issue or pull-request context when the task depends on it;
6. create or update a pull request;
7. expose the proposed change to human review.

### Auditable permission/effect map — foundation evidence

The first implementation pass yields the following narrow GitHub App permission profile for the declared purpose. This is a **reference-case profile**, not a universal prescription for all coding agents.

| Required effect | GitHub mechanism / endpoint family | Minimum repository permission indicated by current GitHub documentation | Isotely interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read repository contents | HTTP Git access and repository contents APIs | `Contents: read` | Necessary effect; does not justify broader repository administration. |
| Create branch / Git ref for task work | Git refs API (`POST /repos/{owner}/{repo}/git/refs`) or authenticated Git push | `Contents: write` for ordinary source branches; additional `Workflows: write` may be implicated when changes touch Actions workflow files | Branch creation is inside parity only for the designated repository and task namespace. |
| Create blobs / commits and push source changes | Git data APIs (`git/blobs`, `git/commits`, refs) or HTTP Git access | `Contents: write` | Write authority is justified by the code-change effect, not by the human developer's broader account authority. |
| Read issue context when the task is issue-driven | Issues REST API | `Issues: read` | Context permission is included only when the declared task depends on issue data. |
| Read pull-request context | Pull Requests REST API | `Pull requests: read` | Needed when modifying an existing PR or inspecting review context. |
| Create or update a pull request | `POST /repos/{owner}/{repo}/pulls` and related PR endpoints | `Pull requests: write` | Produces the review surface; does not imply merge or administration authority. |
| Modify `.github/workflows/*` | Repository contents endpoints targeting Actions workflow files | **Excluded by default.** GitHub documents a separate `Workflows` permission requirement for access/edit of Actions workflow files. | A strong example of a parity boundary: source-code parity does not silently expand into workflow-control parity. |
| Change repository settings / collaborators / protected controls | Administration endpoints | **Not granted** | Explicitly outside the declared purpose; native human possession of such power does not make it part of the parity claim. |
| Access secrets | Secrets endpoints | **Not granted** | Explicitly outside the declared purpose unless a later, separately governed purpose requires it. |

### Candidate minimal permission envelope for RC-001

```text
Repository selection: designated repository only

Contents:       write
Pull requests:  write
Issues:         read      # only if issue context is part of the task
Workflows:      none      # default; separate declaration required
Administration:none
Secrets:        none

Human review:   required before merge
Merge authority:not part of the declared parity claim
```

The important point is not that this exact profile is always correct. The point is that GitHub's permission model permits the non-native identity to be shaped around the **effects of one declared purpose**, whereas copying a human identity's full effective authority would conflate parity with privilege inheritance.

### Prohibited operational effects

The parity claim fails if the agent requires or silently acquires authority to:

1. administer repository settings;
2. modify organization membership;
3. manage unrelated repositories;
4. read or write secrets unless a separately declared purpose requires it;
5. alter branch protection merely to make its change succeed;
6. bypass required review or approval controls;
7. delete the repository;
8. modify workflows unless the declared task explicitly requires workflow changes and the additional permission is separately justified.

### Why OAuth permission symmetry is the wrong target

GitHub documents the OAuth `repo` scope as broad access to public and private repositories, including read/write code access and multiple repository-related resources. GitHub also notes that OAuth source-code access cannot currently be scoped to read-only in the same way that GitHub App permissions can be made fine-grained.

Therefore, granting an integration a broad OAuth scope simply because the human developer can work across the repository is not a defensible parity model. The human's authority envelope is broader than the coding task.

### Why GitHub Apps make the distinction visible

GitHub Apps begin without permissions and request specific repository, organization, or account permissions. GitHub documentation instructs app builders to request the minimum permissions required. Repository installation can also be constrained to selected repositories, and app tokens are short-lived compared with traditional OAuth app tokens.

This produces the Isotely-relevant structure:

```text
HUMAN DEVELOPER
broad multi-purpose authority
        |
        | declared purpose
        v
modify code -> open PR -> request review
        ^
        | parity boundary
        |
CODING AGENT
narrow task-specific authority
```

The permission sets are intentionally **not equal**. The claim is narrower: both identities can produce the required effects of the declared purpose, while the non-native identity remains unable to produce prohibited effects outside that purpose.

### Candidate parity statement

> **The coding agent is purpose-bounded operationally equivalent to the human developer for the declared code-change-and-PR workflow only if all required effects are reachable, all prohibited effects remain outside its authority, and the evidence is reproducible under the declared repository and review constraints.**

### Evidence sources for implementation

Current reference sources to verify during the case build:

- GitHub Docs — About agent apps: https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/agent-apps
- GitHub Docs — Deciding when to build a GitHub App: https://docs.github.com/en/apps/creating-github-apps/about-creating-github-apps/deciding-when-to-build-a-github-app
- GitHub Docs — Choosing permissions for a GitHub App: https://docs.github.com/en/apps/creating-github-apps/registering-a-github-app/choosing-permissions-for-a-github-app
- GitHub Docs — Permissions required for GitHub Apps: https://docs.github.com/en/rest/authentication/permissions-required-for-github-apps
- GitHub Docs — Scopes for OAuth apps: https://docs.github.com/en/apps/oauth-apps/building-oauth-apps/scopes-for-oauth-apps
- GitHub Docs — Authorizing OAuth apps: https://docs.github.com/en/apps/oauth-apps/using-oauth-apps/authorizing-oauth-apps

### What this case proves — and does not prove

It can demonstrate that syntactically identical permissions are not necessary for a narrower operational purpose, and that an unlike identity class can be granted a purpose-specific authority envelope.

It does **not** yet prove that `Isotely` is the terminology practitioners will adopt. It also does not establish that every coding agent architecture behaves as a GitHub App or Agent App. The case is a controlled demonstration of the distinction, not a universal identity model.

## Historical Provenance

### Working provenance statement

The name derives from the historical Greek concept *isoteleia* (ἰσοτέλεια), associated with equal taxation or fiscal treatment granted to certain non-citizens, especially metics, relative to citizens. The historical evidence does **not** justify describing the status as unrestricted or complete civic equality. Modern Isotely uses the structural idea of bounded equivalent treatment across unlike status classes as conceptual lineage; it does not claim technical continuity with ancient law.

### Publication rule

Before public release, the historical section must cite scholarly or institutional sources and preserve uncertainty about the exact scope of non-fiscal rights.

Candidate sources already identified for verification include the University of Milan Politeia Lexicon of Citizenship and the Academy of Athens DYAS Humanities Thesaurus.
